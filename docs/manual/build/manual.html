Getting started

Your on-ramp to AI Creative Center: what it is, what your machine needs, how to install and launch it, how editions unlock features, and how to find your way around the interface.

Welcome to AI Creative Center — a complete, self-contained creative studio that generates images, video, music, voices, 3D models, and entire films on your own computer. This chapter gets you from a fresh install to confidently navigating the app. When you're ready to create, jump to the module chapters: Images, Video, Audio & voice, Music, 3D, and Cinema — AI Film Studio.

What AI Creative Center is

AI Creative Center is a 100% local, offline desktop application. Every generation — every image, frame of video, note of music, and 3D mesh — is computed on your own GPU. Nothing you create is uploaded to a cloud service, and no account or internet connection is needed to use the app.

There are only two optional moments where the app touches the network, and both are under your control:

  1. One-time model-weight downloads — the first time you use a module, its AI model weights may need to be fetched. Once downloaded, they live on your disk and are reused offline forever after. The setup wizard handles this; see Wizards.
  2. Civitai import (Studio Pro only) — the Studio Pro edition can search and download community models from Civitai. This is an opt-in feature of the Import models tab, not something the app does on its own.

Everything else — including the language you read this in, the models you run, and the files you produce — stays on your machine.

System requirements & GPU / VRAM notes

AI Creative Center runs on Windows with an NVIDIA GPU. The single most important factor for what you can do — and how fast — is your GPU's VRAM.

  • Image generation is the lightest workload and runs on modest cards.
  • Video, 3D, and film are the heaviest and reward more VRAM (24 GB is comfortable for the largest models).
  • The app measures your GPU's memory without ever creating a CUDA context in the interface process, so the main window stays stable even when heavy sub-processes are running.

Running on limited VRAM (quantization)

You do not need a top-tier card to run large models. AI Creative Center supports several quantized weight formats that trade a small amount of quality for dramatically lower memory use, and it can auto-select the right format for your VRAM:

Format Typical VRAM Quality vs. full precision Good for
NVFP4 (mixed) ~14 GB Almost identical 32 GB cards (fastest)
FP8 scaled (mixed) ~16–18 GB Almost identical Universal compromise
GGUF Q8_0 14–16 GB 98–99% 16 GB cards
GGUF Q6_K 11–13 GB ~95% 12 GB cards
GGUF Q5_K_M 9–11 GB ~90% 8 GB cards (sweet spot)
GGUF Q4_K_M 7–9 GB 75–85% 8 / 6 GB cards

VRAM-saving techniques (text-encoder offload, VAE offload, block-swap, model unloading between jobs) stack on top of quantization. For a full walkthrough of running the largest image model on constrained hardware, see docs/FLUX2_LOW_VRAM.md. You can also cap the memory the app is allowed to use and let it place models automatically — see VRAM calibration in Advanced settings.

Installation & first launch

  1. Run the installer for your platform and follow the prompts. The installer is selective: it can link the models you already have on disk and download only the weights your edition actually needs, keeping the footprint small.
  2. Launch AI Creative Center. On first run, the setup wizard opens automatically to connect your local models and fetch any missing weights. Follow it end to end — it is the fastest path to a working install. Full details are in Wizards.
  3. When the wizard finishes, the main window opens and you are ready to create.

You can re-open the wizard at any time from the Tools ▸ Wizard menu.

Images

Generate images from text, edit them with inpaint/outpaint, recolor and upscale, cut objects out with AI selection, and impose real faces — all around a single shared canvas.

The Images tab (tab.images) is the heart of still-image creation in AI Creative Center. It is organised as four sub-tabs down the left — Generate image, Inpaint / Outpaint, Color, and Definition (upscale) — plus an always-visible mask canvas on the right that every operation draws from. Below the canvas sits the AI selection panel (SAM segmentation), and the Generate sub-tab adds the Face adapter (PuLID) and Compose multiple photos panels.

How image generation works

An image model turns your text prompt into pixels through a number of diffusion steps. You choose an architecture (FLUX, Z-Image or Ernie), a specific model file, and optionally a text encoder and VAE — or leave those on auto and let the app pick the matching pair. Two extra dials shape the result: CFG (how strictly the image obeys the prompt) and the seed (the random starting point; fix it to reproduce a result exactly).

The editing sub-tabs work differently from generation:

  • Inpaint / Outpaint use a mask (drawn on the canvas) to mark the area to regenerate or the borders to extend, using a Fill-type model.
  • Integrate and Compose are edit-by-reference operations (Kontext / FLUX.2): the extra images are handed to the model as references, not pasted, and the model harmonizes the scene. No mask is needed for these — only for Inpaint/Outpaint.
  • Recolor changes the colour of a described subject from the prompt alone.

All model weights are downloaded once from Hugging Face; nothing runs in the cloud. See Import models for managing them.

Video

Turn a prompt, a photo, or an audio track into moving pictures with LTX-2 — then guide motion with ControlNet, extend clips to any length, edit them with AI, re-shoot sections, and lip-sync speech.

The Video tab (tab.video) generates video from text (t2v), from a starting image (i2v), or from an audio track (a2v). It shares its prompt, model selector, LoRA panel and sampler/seed group with several companion tabs that specialize in editing and post-production: ControlNet, Extend video, AI Video Edit, plus the in-tab V2V panel (Normal / Talk / Sing / ReTake) and Storyboard for multi-scene shots.

How video generation works

You write a prompt (and optionally a negative prompt), pick a preset that sets resolution, FPS, duration and step count, choose a model, and press generate. LTX-2 samples the video over a number of steps using the LTXVScheduler with CFG 1.0 — the guidance is provided by NAG, so you don't raise CFG the way you would for images.

  • Text-to-video (t2v): prompt only.
  • Image-to-video (i2v): add a guide image; the i2v method and image strength control how the video starts from it.
  • Audio-to-video (a2v): attach an audio track from the Audio & voice tab; the model animates in time with it.

Type \ in any prompt field to open the tag palette (pauses, emotions, speed). Advanced sampler, scheduler and SHIFT controls live in the embedded Sampler/Seed group and the Advanced settings tab.

Troubleshooting

  • The video degenerates into "dots" or dematerializes on native resolution. This is a known LTXVScheduler shift issue on native token counts — adjust Base Shift in the Sampler/Seed group, and don't force high CFG (keep 1.0).
  • CFG has no effect / high CFG looks over-cooked. On LTX, guidance comes from NAG — leave CFG at 1.0 and change the preset/steps instead.

Audio & Voice

Turn text into natural speech, clone a voice from a short sample, and attach a spoken track to any video — all locally, with your choice of seven speech engines.

The Audio / Voice tab (tab.audio_voice) is where you decide what your videos say and how they say it. It combines three things in one place: a source selector that chooses where a video's audio comes from, a full multi-engine text-to-speech (TTS) studio, and a shared voice library that stores custom and cloned voices for reuse across the whole application (Video, Music Video, and the AI Film Studio all draw voices from this same library).

How audio works in AI Creative Center

When you generate a video, the model can produce sound in several ways. The Audio source radio group at the top of the tab picks exactly one of them:

  • Native LTX audio — the video model (LTX-2) invents the audio itself, driven by your video prompt. Good for ambient sound and simple spoken lines described in the prompt.
  • Audio I provide — you supply a ready-made track: a file from disk, a live microphone recording, or a clip you synthesized with the built-in TTS and sent over with Use as video audio.
  • Clone voice — you pick a saved voice (with its reference sample) from the library. With text in the TTS box, that text is synthesized in the chosen voice; with the text left empty, only the voice timbre is handed to LTX, which then generates the spoken lines from your video prompt.
  • Mute — no audio at all.

The TTS studio below the source selector works independently: you can synthesize, preview, and reuse speech at any time, whether or not you are making a video.

Music Video

Turn a song into a finished music video: AI Creative Center writes a shot list from your lyrics, generates an opening image and a video clip for each shot, then concatenates everything and adds the audio track.

The Music Video Studio is a project-based workflow. Each project is saved to disk, so you can build a video in stages, close the app, and come back to exactly where you left off. The Studio walks through four phases — Song, Shots/Images, Videos, Assembly — each of which you can review and edit before moving on.

Edition: Full+ · Capability: cap.music_video. Studio Pro adds nothing extra here except model import.

The whole pipeline runs locally with native engines (no cloud): a local language model plans the storyboard from your lyrics, the image engine renders each opening frame, and the LTX-2 video engine animates it into a clip.

How the workflow fits together

  1. Song — Generate (or regenerate) the track with HeartMuLa. All songs made for the project are listed; you pick the active one.
  2. Shots / Images — A local LLM reads your lyrics and builds a shot list, distributing shots across the song's duration. You edit the list — reorder, add, delete, adjust timing and prompts — then generate an opening image per shot.
  3. Videos — Each opening image is animated into an image-to-video clip. You can regenerate, reorder or delete individual clips.
  4. Assembly — The clips are concatenated in order and the active song's audio is muxed in, producing the final music_video.mp4.

Because the shots are separate image-to-video clips, scene changes are clean hard cuts by design; optional transitions can soften them at assembly time.

The projects manager (Music video projects dialog) lists every saved project with its current phase and song count. From here you can 📂 Open, 📋 Duplicate, or 🗑 Delete a project. Opening one launches the Studio at its saved phase.

Continuity engine

The continuity engine makes consistency an explicit state, not a lucky side effect of prompting.

Per-scene / per-shot state fields

Field Default Options / Notes
Time of day day day, night, dawn, dusk, golden hour
Weather clear clear, cloudy, rain, snow, fog, storm
Lighting natural soft natural soft, hard sun, low key, high key, candlelight, neon
Color grade neutral narrative color grade
Mood neutral neutral, tense, joyful, melancholic, epic, romantic, scary
Lens (empty) 24mm wide, 35mm, 50mm, 85mm portrait, anamorphic — persists across scenes
Film look (empty) warm cinematic, cool teal-orange, bleach bypass, vintage film grain, high contrast noir, pastel — persists
Per-asset state — for each @character: variation, wardrobe, condition (e.g. wounded, wet), emotion

How it works - Forward propagation — state propagates forward from scene to scene and shot to shot. Photographic choices (lens, film look) persist; character changes (e.g. an actor gets muddy in scene 2) carry until you change them. - Prompt injection — the relevant state is composed into a phrase (time, weather, lighting, color grade, mood, lens, film look, plus each asset's condition/emotion/wardrobe) and injected into every image/video prompt, so you never re-type it. - Warnings — the engine flags incoherences (e.g. time of day changes: day → night, or a character's condition lost without reason) so you can catch jumps before rendering.

To edit continuity, select a scene in the storyboard, set the fields in the Scene continuity panel, and press Apply + propagate. Use 👤 Character state… to set wardrobe / condition / emotion per character; it is saved and propagated to following scenes.

Generation service

Under the hood, two native functions do the actual rendering. You never call them directly, but their parameters explain what the UI controls map to.

gen_image — renders one image: - prompt, out_png, width (1024), height (1024), seed (7) - face_ref → PuLID face conditioning (strength 0.85) · ref_image / ref_images → Kontext multi-reference (used when there is no face_ref) - loras (optional) · arch (flux) · model (checkpoint) · negative (extends the default negative with continuity's forbidden elements) - Internal defaults: 22 steps, guidance 3.5.

Training Your Own LoRA

Teach the app new faces, characters, objects, places and styles by training your own LoRA adapters — from a simple images folder, or from a dataset the app builds for you automatically.

A LoRA is a small "add-on" file that steers a base model toward a specific subject or look. Once trained, a LoRA plugs into image and video generation everywhere in AI Creative Center: you type its trigger word in a prompt and the subject appears, consistently, take after take.

This chapter covers the three training tools the app offers, from the most hands-on to the most automated:

Tool Where What you supply Output Edition

The classification pipeline

Every imported model is analyzed automatically so it lands in the right folder with the right metadata. The analysis fills in these fields:

Field Meaning
type LoRA · Checkpoint · VAE · TextEncoder · Embedding · ControlNet · Upscaler …
family Flux · SDXL · SD1.5 · SD3 · LTX · Wan21 · Wan22 · Z-Image · Ernie …
dest_subfolder Where the file is placed, e.g. LoRA/Flux.
sha256 The file's hash, used for a Civitai by-hash lookup.
triggers Trained trigger words (activation keywords).
preview A preview image URL.
base_arch The base architecture reported by Civitai.

How type and family are determined — The app first inspects the file itself (by structure and size) to classify type and family. If that is inconclusive, it falls back to the native model detector, which maps internal codes (for example flux1/flux2 → Flux, sdxl → SDXL, ltxv → LTX, wan21 → Wan21) to standard names. For .safetensors/.ckpt/.pt files it also computes the SHA-256 and queries Civitai by hash; a match overrides the guessed type/family and supplies triggers, a preview, and the base model.

Content rating (adult-content gating)

AI Creative Center classifies each model's content rating through a cascade, stopping at the first confident source. This is what drives whether a model is treated as 18+/adult content:

  1. Civitai metadata (authoritative) — if the model comes from Civitai, its rating flags are used directly.
  2. Name/tag heuristic — otherwise, the filename and tags are scanned for adult-content hints.
  3. Preview classifier — if a preview image is available, an on-device classifier inspects it.
  4. Conservative default — if still uncertain, the model is treated as adult content (the safe default).

The Unlocked (18+) mode (cap.adult_mode) governs what happens next. When it is off, any model rated as adult content is skipped during import (the log notes "Skipped (adult content, feature not enabled)"), and the adult-content checkboxes in Discover and Library are disabled. When it is on, adult-rated models import and browse normally. You can always correct a model's rating by hand in the Library detail view.

How presets work in this app (read this first)

There is one rule that governs every "preset" and "optimize" action in AI Creative Center, and it will save you confusion:

Presets write into the GUI controls — they do not silently reconfigure the pipeline.

When you pick a quality preset, the app fills in the Steps and CFG fields (and, for the Video-tab presets, resolution / FPS / duration). Those fields then remain fully editable by hand. Nothing is hidden: what you see in the controls is exactly what the model receives. This means you can start from a preset and then fine-tune a single value without fighting an invisible override.

The Sampler / Seed group

Edition: Full+ · Capability: cap.video (video sampling)

What it does — Controls how many denoising steps run, how strongly the prompt is enforced (CFG), which sampler and scheduler are used, the LTX SHIFT quality lever, which text encoder reads your prompt, whether the prompt is auto-enhanced, and the random seed.

Where to find it — This group (internally the Sampler / Seed group) is embedded directly in the Video tab, and its settings are documented here because they are the "advanced" heart of video generation. The heading reads Sampler / Seed.

Step by step 1. (Optional) Choose a Quality (preset): entry to fill Steps and CFG with a known-good combination. 2. Adjust Steps and CFG if you want finer control. 3. Leave Sampler / Sched. on the defaults unless you have a specific reason to change them. 4. Tune the Shift (LTX) base / max values — this is the highest-impact, zero-cost video quality lever. 5. Tips for best results

  • Leave CFG at 1.0 and Scheduler on LTXVScheduler (shift) unless you have a concrete problem to solve — this is the verified reference pipeline.
  • To sharpen a soft result at zero extra cost, adjust the SHIFT base: lower it slightly for more fine detail, raise it for steadier motion and less flicker.
  • Use Fixed seed while dialing in a shot so that each change you make is the only variable.
  • Turn the prompt enhancer off once your prompts are detailed; it can steer short prompts helpfully but adds its own interpretation.

Tips for best results

  • Keep the single-pass threshold at 20 s — that is LTX-2.3's native ceiling; pushing it higher degrades the base pass.
  • If faces slowly drift over a long clip, first raise Scene anti-drift to ~0.25; only if that is not enough, set Re-anchor face every to 2–4.
  • Raise context strength toward 1.0 for locked-down, low-motion scenes; lower it for dynamic action where you want more movement.
  • Leave Resume after crash on for long overnight renders so a single interruption doesn't cost you the whole video.

Invisible Provenance Signature

How AI Creative Center invisibly signs everything it produces so you can later prove it was AI‑generated — and how to control it.

Every image and every video frame produced by AI Creative Center carries an invisible provenance signature. It is a robust, imperceptible watermark that embeds the brand mark AI Tools Forge directly into the pixels, so the origin of a file can be verified even after it has been screenshotted, resized, compressed, or re‑photographed. The signature does not alter the picture in any way you can see.

Edition: All editions · Capability: cap.stego (default ON, user‑disableable)

This chapter explains what the signature is, how to turn it on or off, how to add an optional short text tag, and how the standalone ATF Detector reads it back. See also Getting started and Advanced settings.

What it is

The provenance signature is a spread‑spectrum watermark in the DCT (frequency) domain. Instead of writing a visible mark in one corner, it spreads a very low‑amplitude, pseudo‑random pattern across the whole picture. That pattern is generated from a secret key, so only a matching detector can find it — no visible logo, no metadata that a re‑export would strip.

It is designed to be:

  • Invisible — the signal sits in the mid‑frequency band, below the threshold of human perception.
  • Redundant — it is repeated over an 8×8 grid of tiles, so it survives partial cropping.
  • Robust — each tile is normalized to a canonical size before analysis, so the mark survives resizing, JPEG compression, screenshots, and photos of a screen.
  • Anti‑averaging (video) — the pattern changes on every frame, so averaging frames together does not reveal a fixed mark to remove.
  • Blind — verification needs only the secret key, never the original file.

Honest note. No invisible watermark is literally impossible to remove. This one is strongly resistant to random edits, frame averaging, and screenshots; a determined attacker with the secret key could still target it. The signature is a provenance aid, not a DRM lock.

Turn it on or off (and add a text tag)

Where to find it — Settings → Provenance (ui/settings_dialog.py, the Provenance group).

Step by step

  1. Open Settings.
  2. Find the Provenance group.
  3. Toggle "Invisible provenance signature (recommended)" — checked by default.
  4. Optionally, type up to five characters into "Signature text (max 5)" (for example, your own short handle or code). Leave it empty for a signature with no text — it is still fully valid.
  5. Save. The setting takes effect on the next generation.

Parameters

Control Type Default Range / Options What it does & when to change

Signature text (max 5) Text field Empty 0–5 characters Optional short tag stamped invisibly alongside the presence mark and read back by the ATF Detector. Empty = signature without text. Five characters is the physical limit for a screenshot‑robust invisible mark; for longer text, use the file's own metadata instead.

It is the only reliable way to later prove one of your renders came from this software. - Keep the text tag short and meaningful — an initials handle or short ID. If you only need to prove "this was AI‑generated with AI Tools Forge," the presence mark alone (empty text) is enough.

Technical parameters

For advanced users and integrators, these are the fixed constants of the watermark (core/stego.py). You never set these in the app; they are listed here for transparency and for anyone integrating the ATF Detector.

Constant Value Meaning
Grid 8 × 8 (64 tiles) Redundant impression sites; survives partial crops.
Canonical tile size 80 px Each tile is normalized to this size before DCT — the source of resize robustness.
Nonce bank 24 Number of per‑frame carrier variants the detector tries.
Presence amplitude (alpha) ~9.0 Tuned for invisibility (~PSNR 39, max pixel delta ~9). Higher values would be visible ("blotchy").
Detection z‑threshold 5.0 Presence is declared above this z‑score (false‑positive rate ~1e‑6).
Payload 5 chars + CRC8 = 48 bits Optional text tag with an integrity checksum.
Payload amplitude (beta) 1.2 Kept low to preserve invisibility; the priority is a clean image.
DCT band mid‑band, r ∈ [4, 26] Survives resize/JPEG/screenshot; DC and high frequencies excluded.
Perceptual mask Laplacian‑based, per‑pixel Hides the signal in local detail/texture (~100%) and nearly zeroes it on smooth areas like skin and sky (~8%).
Per‑frame nonce yes (video) The carrier changes every frame so averaging frames reveals no fixed pattern.

The key is derived deterministically from an SHA‑256 stream, so the same signature can be produced and verified identically across Python/NumPy and JavaScript — which is how the mobile companion and on‑device detector can read the same mark.

Robustness

The signature is engineered to survive the ways files actually get shared and re‑captured:

  • Resizing — the grid‑to‑image ratio scales with the image, so the mid‑band survives resampling.
  • JPEG / re‑compression — the mid‑band is chosen precisely to survive lossy compression.
  • Screenshots — normalization to the canonical tile size keeps the mark readable after a screen capture.
  • Photo of a screen — the robust detector performs a geometric re‑sync that compensates for a slightly rotated or perspective‑skewed re‑photograph.
  • Perspective / mild warping — the robust path searches over small rotations (±15°) and scales (0.90–1.10) to re‑align the grid.

The hardest case remains a strongly skewed photo‑of‑screen with moiré; even then the presence mark usually holds while the optional text tag may be lost.

The ATF Detector

The ATF Detector is the standalone tool that reads the signature back. It works blind — it needs only the secret key, never your original file — and it returns four values:

  • found — whether the signature was detected.
  • z‑score — the confidence of the detection.
  • nonce — which per‑frame carrier variant matched.
  • text — the optional 5‑character tag, if one was embedded and its checksum verifies.

Two modes are available (core/stego.py):

Function Use Threshold
detect_image Fast blind detection, no geometric re‑sync — good for clean files. z ≥ 5.0
detect_image_robust Full geometric re‑sync (rotation × scale search) for skewed photos of a screen. z ≥ 6.5

The robust path uses a higher threshold (6.5) because searching over many geometric warps inflates the maximum correlation under the null hypothesis. For files, detect_file runs the robust detector on images and samples several frames of a video, keeping the strongest result.

Tips for best results - Use the robust detector for anything that has been photographed off a screen or heavily edited; use the fast detector for original files. - A high z‑score with no recovered text simply means the presence mark survived but the optional tag did not — the file is still proven AI‑generated. - Because detection is blind and key‑derived, you can verify third‑party copies of your work without keeping the originals.

Edition: All editions (edition‑aware download)

Local REST API

Automate every creative function in AI Creative Center from your own scripts, the mobile companion, or an AI agent — all on your own machine, nothing in the cloud.

Edition: Studio Pro only · Capability: api.rest

The local REST API exposes every native function of AI Creative Center — images, video, film shots, music, voice, 3D, world generation, and LoRA training — as asynchronous jobs that return download links. It is the same engine that powers the mobile companion app and the multi‑agent skill packs. Everything runs on http://127.0.0.1:8200; no data leaves your computer.

See also Advanced settings and the capability table.

Starting and stopping the server

  • Autostart — Run python -m api.autostart. It starts the server if it is down, waits for /health, and prints READY http://127.0.0.1:8200 when ready. The agent skill packs call this at the start of a session.
  • Manual start — python -m api.app.
  • Auto‑shutdown — After 30 minutes with no requests and no jobs queued or running, the server shuts itself down. The next call simply re‑launches it via autostart.
  • Interactive docs — Swagger UI at http://127.0.0.1:8200/docs; the OpenAPI schema at /openapi.json.

Environment variables: LTX_API_HOST (default 127.0.0.1), LTX_API_PORT (default 8200), LTX_API_IDLE_TIMEOUT (default 1800 seconds), LTX_API_PYTHON (the Python interpreter that runs the API).

The asynchronous job model

Every creative action is a POST with a JSON body that returns a job id immediately; you then poll for the result and download the files. GPU jobs run one at a time — do not fire jobs in parallel.

  1. Submit — POST an action → { "job_id": "...", "status": "pending" }.
  2. Poll — GET /jobs/{job_id} (back off 2–5 s) until status is success or error:
  3. running → reports progress (0–1) and a message.
  4. pending → reports queue_position.
  5. success → returns files: [{ path, name, download_url }].
  6. error → returns an error message (often a missing prerequisite to install).
  7. Download — GET /download?path=<path> (served only from inside output/ and output/api_uploads/).

Chaining — Upload a file once with POST /files/upload (multipart, field file) and reuse the returned path in any *_path field (image_path, video_path, audio_path, mask_path, mesh_path). A job's output path is a local path on the server, so you can feed it straight into the next step with no intermediate download.

Gating: 403 with upsell

Each endpoint checks the same capability the desktop app checks. If your edition does not include a capability, the endpoint returns HTTP 403 with upsell information (the minimum edition and how to unlock it) rather than running. Tips & Tricks

A skimmable, cross‑module guide to getting the best results — the settings that matter, and the traps to avoid.

This chapter consolidates the practical advice scattered through the app's tooltips, presets, and internal notes. Each section is a domain you can jump to. For per‑feature detail, follow the cross‑links to Images, Quality & restore, Audio & voice, Music, 3D, and Advanced settings.

Images

  • Choose the scheduler deliberately — it changes image quality a lot. For FLUX images, the flow‑aware schedulers bong_tangent / kl_optimal / beta57 are often the best (high quality). karras gives classic detail, beta suits distilled models, and exponential is softer.
  • Sampler: euler and dpmpp are reliable. res_2s gives more detail on FLUX images — but never use it for LTX video, where it breaks (see Video below).
  • Face identity from a photo: with PuLID, keep the strength around 0.8 (recommended range 0.6–0.9). If faces look plastic, use a base bf16 model rather than a distilled/quantized one, and push the reference/image strength higher.
  • Generate at full resolution. Leave any half‑resolution / downscale option off for maximum native detail; it only hides the VAE decoder grid at the cost of softness.
  • Guidance: FLUX text‑to‑image sits comfortably around guidance 3.5; inpaint/fill wants much higher guidance (~30) to fill cleanly.

Video

  • Use the verified sampler. The default euler_ancestral_cfg_pp with LTXVScheduler (shift) and CFG 1.0 is the tuned, verified LTX‑2.3 pipeline — the guidance comes from NAG, not from a high CFG.
  • Avoid RES samplers on video. res_2s, res_multistep, and similar look great on paper for flow but produce green noise on LTX‑2.3 (verified A/B) — they were removed for exactly this reason. If you see cyan/green artifacts, you are on the wrong sampler.
  • The base shift is the #1 video quality lever. In LTXVScheduler, a higher base shift (default 0.95) spends more steps on structure and motion (temporal coherence, less flicker); a lower shift gives finer per‑frame detail but risks inconsistency between frames.
  • Image‑to‑video "image strength" controls motion feel. Set the soft‑reference image strength to 0.45–0.60 for natural motion; 0.80+ sticks tightly to the first frame but makes the motion stiff.
  • Use the built‑in presets as starting points: ⚡ FAST (8 steps, reduced res — a draft to validate the idea, ~1 min), 🎯 PRO (24 steps, full 1280×704 — best balance), 💎 HQ (44 steps, no cache — maximum quality, slowest).
  • Long videos are chunked automatically. Clips over ~150 frames are segmented into memory‑bounded chunks and stitched, so you can request long durations without hitting out‑of‑memory.
  • TeaCache caveat. With the raw L1 metric (no LTX‑2.3 coefficients) the aggressive thresholds skip almost everything — fast but destructive (PSNR collapses). Presets ship it at 0.0; only nudge the manual slider up if you have validated a low threshold on your own content.

Audio & Voice

  • Match the TTS engine to the job. Use a lightweight engine (edge/kokoro) for fast narration and a clone‑capable engine (f5tts/xtts2/fish/vibevoice/cosyvoice2) when you supply a voice sample. Provide the sample_transcript for the best clone fidelity.
  • Separate vocals before lip‑sync when your source audio has music under the speech — cleaner vocals give tighter lip motion.
  • Lip‑sync strength: aim for 1.5–3.0 on the sync scale. 1.0 is weak; above 3 the mouth motion becomes exaggerated.

Music

  • Sampler matters for lyric/style adherence. For the music engine, pingpong (SDE) gives the best coherence and style/lyric alignment (recommended when you need the lyrics honored); euler is fast and standard; heun is more precise but slower.
  • Guidance: the ACE‑Step engine defaults to guidance ~15.0 — a strong pull toward your tags and lyrics.
  • Write descriptive tags. The style tags (genre, instruments, mood) drive the result as much as the lyrics do.

3D

  • Feed a clean, well‑lit single subject for image→3D; busy backgrounds and occlusions hurt mesh quality.
  • Tune the mesh trade‑offs: more ss_steps/slat_steps (default 12) improves quality at the cost of time; simplify (default 0.95) and texture_size (default 1024) trade detail for file size.
  • Rigging expects a clean mesh; convert to FBX/OBJ afterward via the built‑in Blender step if your target tool needs it.

Film Studio

  • Keep character assets consistent by reusing the same face reference (PuLID) and LoRA across shots.
  • Generate asset images first, then shots — the shot video step can take keyframes and LoRA to keep continuity.
  • Heavy project orchestration (scheduler, editing, dubbing, Auto mode) is stateful and lives in the GUI; use it rather than scripting each shot by hand.

Performance / VRAM

  • Pick a weight format for your GPU. On 32 GB, NVFP4 mixed is fastest (~14 GB, ~2× faster than BF16, almost no visible difference). FP8 Scaled is the best universal compromise. For smaller cards, GGUF quants scale down: Q8_0 (16 GB, 98–99% quality), Q6_K (12 GB, ~95%), Q5_K_M (8 GB sweet spot, ~90%), Q4_K_M (6–8 GB, 75–85%).
  • VRAM savings stack multiplicatively: text‑encoder offload after encoding, VAE offload except during decode, transformer block‑swap, and unloading models between generations.
  • Use /free when a video job risks OOM — it releases held VRAM before the next run.
  • Slow first run is normal. The first time you open a heavy module the app downloads its on‑demand weights; subsequent runs are fast.
  • Use the prompt enhancer to expand terse prompts into richer descriptions before generating, especially for video where detailed motion cues help coherence.

Symptom Cause Fix
Out of memory (OOM) Resolution/steps too high, or a large unquantized model. Lower resolution or steps; switch to a quantized model (fp8/fp4/gguf); use /free to release VRAM before the next job.
Very slow first run On‑demand weights are downloading. Wait for the one‑time download to finish; later runs are fast.
Green / cyan noise in video Wrong sampler for LTX. Use euler_ancestral_cfg_pp with LTXVScheduler (shift); avoid RES samplers on video.
Plastic‑looking faces Distilled/quantized model, or reference too loose. Use a base bf16 model and raise the image/reference strength (PuLID ~0.8).
